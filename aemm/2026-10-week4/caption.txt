31 October is World Savings Day. In Nepali villages, saving and borrowing often happen between neighbours: a loan to a cousin, help from a friend, a dhukuti group. So what happens when families get a bank account of their own?

Economists Margherita Comola and Silvia Prina studied 19 villages around Pokhara. A lottery gave some women a free savings account, and the researchers tracked the loans and gifts between neighbours before and after.

The finding? We usually assume that once families can save in a bank, they stop lending to and helping their neighbours. This study found the opposite. More than 84% of women opened and used the account, and a year later their households were lending roughly 50% more to others in the village.

But the extra lending was mostly bigger loans, above Rs 2,400, and gifts did not change. The rise also came mostly from households that were already better off.

Why this matters: Many people worry that banks will replace the trust between neighbours. This study suggests a safe place to save can make families stronger lenders instead. Poorer families may need more help to gain the same way. This raises a bigger question about how banks and village support can work together.
