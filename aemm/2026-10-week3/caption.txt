Dashain brings families home, fills courtyards with jamara and swings, and fills markets with shoppers. But can the timing of a festival shape how rich a place becomes over time?

Economists Eduardo Montero and Dean Yang studied towns in Mexico, where each town celebrates its own patron saint on a fixed day. In some towns that day falls in the busiest weeks of planting or harvest. In others it does not.

The finding? We usually assume a festival is a few days of joy and spending, with no lasting effect on a town's income. This study found the date matters. Towns whose festival lands in planting or harvest season have about 20% lower income per person today.

Why? Farm work suffered in the busiest weeks, and over centuries these towns moved more slowly into better-paid jobs outside farming. But the same towns are also more religious and more closely knit, and the authors do not say they are worse off overall.

Why this matters: Dashain comes close to rice harvest time in much of Nepal, and many workers travel home for it. Festivals build trust and togetherness, which have real value. The question is how to celebrate together without losing the harvest.
