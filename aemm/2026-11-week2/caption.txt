On 30 October, the economist William Nordhaus passed away at 85. He won the 2018 Nobel Prize for showing how climate change and the economy are tied together. Today we remember him with one of his most loved papers.

Nordhaus asked a simple question: how many hours of work did it take to buy light? He traced it from campfires and clay oil lamps to candles, kerosene lamps, light bulbs and modern energy-saving bulbs.

The finding? We usually assume official price figures tell us how much better off we have become. This study found they missed most of the story. Light became so much cheaper that the usual numbers understated the drop by about 900 to 1,600 times.

In 1800, candlelight equal to one bright bulb burning for an hour cost 5 to 7 hours of work. By 1992, the same light cost less than half a second of work.

Why this matters: Think of Nepali homes moving from the smoky kerosene tuki to LED bulbs, or the end of long load-shedding hours. Life can improve far more than income numbers show. Nordhaus also taught the world to put a price on carbon pollution, a lesson that matters for Nepal's melting glaciers. This raises a bigger question about how we measure progress.
